Mortgage prices have skyrocketed since 2020, with the pandemic triggering an unprecedented surge in housing costs. While there has been a slight cooling off since 2024, the damage is done: the average Canadian income of approximately $60,000-$70,000 per year now makes housing unaffordable across most of Canada. What would have been comfortably affordable in 2012 is now out of reach for the majority of working Canadians. In many provinces, even households earning significantly above the national average are spending 40-50% or more of their income on mortgage payments—well beyond the 30% affordability threshold.

How Quickly Has Rent Increased?

The rental market hasn't faired any better, with rent in many cities only steadily decreasing in the 2010's to noticeably climbing across unit types in the past five years. Thus we begin to explore: what factors have lead to the current housing crisis?

Can the People Living in Cities Afford to Rent?

Across these cities, wages have been increasing steadily over time, however rent has been increasing at a much faster rate. In Toronto, wages have increased from 5-20% between 2000 to 2023, and yet rent has increased by 84-180% for various unit types. Even in places like Alberta, where couples saw a 36% increase in income from 2000 to 2023, rent saw a 128-154% increase in rent. As such, a key part of the affordability crisis is that wages have not kept up with increases in rent. This begs a different question however, why has rent gone up by so much?

How Have Rent Responded to Population Changes?

Overall, most years and locations in which there is a high percentage increase in rent also see a corresponding increase in population. There are of course exceptions, such as PEI in 2023 which saw a mere 0.93% increase in average rent despite a 4.52% increase in population, or Ontario in 2022 which saw a 7% increase in average rent with only a 0.27% increase in population. However, positive correlation between population and average rent increases suggests that supply is struggling to keep up with demand, driving up prices.

Out of the 17 cities with the highest rent (above $1500 / month) only one has a vacancy rate above 3%. Looking at the other cities with lower rent, it can be seen that there is an increase in cities with vacancy rates above 3% as rent decreases. As such, it can be seen that there is some correlation between vacancy rates and rent, further indicating there are problems with supply.

Does Housing Construction Keep Up With Population Growth?

At the root of housing issues lies the rate of consutrction of housing units across Canada. In comparison to the sharp increase in population that Canada has seen in the past 5 years, housing completions have remained stagnant. Excluding Newfoundland and Labrador and Saskatchewen, which are notably two of the more affordable provinces, the ratio of completions to population change has been steadily decreasing over the past few years across housing types. Looking at British Columbia and Ontario, two of the least affordable provinces, housing completion ratios have gone under 0.1 in 2023, indicating significantly higher demand than supply and leading to decreased affordability.

In conclusion, the current housing crisis can be seen as a crisis of the government failing Canadians. Construction of housing has failed to keep up with increases in population, which has driven up prices nationwide. Meanwhile, wages have increased at a much slower rate, making housing unnaffordable for an increasing amount of Canadians. In order to stem this crisis, the Canadian government needs to increase housing supply or limit the rate at which the population grows, and should aid in ensuring that wages and housing prices change at proportional rates.
